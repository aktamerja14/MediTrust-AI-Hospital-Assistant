Diabetes Mellitus: Patient Education Guide

What is Diabetes?

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by elevated blood glucose (sugar) levels. This occurs when the body either does not produce enough insulin (Type 1 diabetes) or cannot effectively use the insulin it produces (Type 2 diabetes). Insulin is a hormone produced by the pancreas that allows glucose from food to enter cells for energy.

Types of Diabetes

Type 1 Diabetes
- An autoimmune condition where the immune system attacks insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas
- Usually diagnosed in children, teens, and young adults
- Requires lifelong insulin therapy
- Accounts for approximately 5-10% of diabetes cases

Type 2 Diabetes
- The most common form, accounting for 90-95% of diabetes cases
- Develops when the body becomes resistant to insulin or doesn't produce enough insulin
- Often associated with overweight, physical inactivity, and family history
- Usually diagnosed in adults, but increasingly seen in younger people
- Can often be managed with lifestyle changes, oral medications, and sometimes insulin

Gestational Diabetes
- Develops during pregnancy
- Usually resolves after delivery
- Increases risk of developing Type 2 diabetes later in life

Common Symptoms of Diabetes

The classic symptoms of high blood sugar (hyperglycemia) include:
- Frequent urination (polyuria) - especially at night
- Excessive thirst (polydipsia)
- Unexplained weight loss
- Increased hunger (polyphagia)
- Fatigue and weakness
- Blurred vision
- Slow-healing cuts and bruises
- Frequent infections (skin, gum, bladder, vaginal)
- Tingling or numbness in hands and feet (neuropathy)
- Dry, itchy skin

Note: Some people with Type 2 diabetes may have no symptoms initially. Regular screening is important for at-risk individuals.

Risk Factors for Type 2 Diabetes

- Overweight or obesity (BMI ≥ 25)
- Physical inactivity
- Family history of diabetes
- Age 45 or older
- High blood pressure (≥ 140/90 mmHg)
- Abnormal cholesterol levels (low HDL, high triglycerides)
- History of gestational diabetes
- Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
- Certain ethnic backgrounds (African American, Hispanic, Native American, Asian American)

Diagnosis

Diabetes is diagnosed through blood tests:
- Fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L)
- HbA1c ≥ 6.5% (48 mmol/mol)
- Random plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) with symptoms
- Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) 2-hour glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL

Complications of Uncontrolled Diabetes

Short-term:
- Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) - life-threatening, more common in Type 1
- Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS) - life-threatening, more common in Type 2
- Hypoglycemia (low blood sugar) from medications

Long-term:
- Cardiovascular disease (heart attack, stroke)
- Kidney damage (nephropathy) leading to kidney failure
- Nerve damage (neuropathy) causing pain, numbness, digestive issues
- Eye damage (retinopathy) leading to blindness
- Foot problems potentially leading to amputation
- Skin conditions and infections

Management and Treatment

Lifestyle Modifications (Cornerstone for Type 2)
- Healthy eating: balanced diet with controlled carbohydrates, high fiber, lean proteins
- Regular physical activity: at least 150 minutes/week of moderate exercise
- Weight management: 5-10% weight loss can significantly improve blood sugar control
- Blood glucose monitoring
- Smoking cessation
- Limited alcohol consumption

Medications for Type 2 Diabetes
- Metformin: First-line therapy, improves insulin sensitivity
- Sulfonylureas (glipizide, glimepiride): Stimulate insulin release
- DPP-4 inhibitors (sitagliptin, linagliptin): Increase incretin levels
- SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin): Reduce glucose reabsorption in kidneys
- GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, liraglutide): Enhance insulin secretion, slow gastric emptying
- Insulin therapy: When oral medications are insufficient

Type 1 Diabetes Management
- Multiple daily insulin injections or insulin pump therapy
- Blood glucose monitoring (fingerstick or continuous glucose monitor)
- Carbohydrate counting
- Regular medical follow-up

When to Consult a Doctor

- If you experience any symptoms of diabetes
- If you have risk factors and haven't been screened
- If diagnosed with diabetes and blood sugar is consistently above target
- If you experience symptoms of hypoglycemia (shaking, sweating, confusion, rapid heartbeat)
- If you develop signs of infection, slow-healing wounds, or foot problems
- Before starting new exercise programs or making major diet changes
- Regular check-ups every 3-6 months for monitoring HbA1c, kidney function, eye exams, foot exams

Prevention of Type 2 Diabetes

- Maintain a healthy weight
- Eat a balanced diet rich in whole grains, fruits, vegetables, lean proteins
- Engage in regular physical activity
- Avoid prolonged sedentary behavior
- Get regular health screenings if at risk

Note: This information is for educational purposes only. Always consult a qualified healthcare professional for personalized medical advice, diagnosis, and treatment.
